# AIdventure

---

You came to Briar Sound to write a story. That's what you told your editor, anyway — a human interest piece about a dying Maine fishing town and the development deal that might save it or finish it off. The kind of story that fills Sunday supplements between mattress ads. Easy. Routine. The sort of assignment they give you when they're not sure you can handle anything else anymore, not after what happened in Dunwich last spring, not after the incident report and the mandatory counseling sessions and the three months of nights when you couldn't sleep without the lights on.

Your rental car died a quarter mile from town, just stopped like something had reached into the engine and pinched a nerve, and you walked the rest of the way with your bag over your shoulder and the October wind coming off the bay carrying a smell like copper and cold stone. Briar Sound looked exactly like its photographs — white clapboard, harbor masts, a church on a hill — but something was wrong with the angles, the way the buildings sat against the sky, as if the town had been built by someone working from a photograph of a town rather than any architectural plan. You told yourself it was the light. October light in coastal Maine does strange things.

Now you're sitting in Maisie's Diner with a cup of coffee that tastes like it was brewed in 1987 and never stopped, and Maisie herself is leaning across the counter with an expression you recognize from a hundred interviews — the face of someone who has been waiting a very long time for someone to tell their story to. 'You're a reporter,' she says. It isn't a question. 'Good. There's someone upstairs I need you to meet. She washed up on the Sounding Rocks three days ago and she — ' Maisie pauses, and her hand tightens on the counter until her knuckles go white. 'She knows things she shouldn't. Things about this town. Things about what's under the bay. And honey, I think she knows things about what's coming.' From somewhere above you, through the diner's pressed-tin ceiling, you hear a sound like fingernails dragging across plaster, tracing shapes you can almost see behind your closed eyes.

> *Go upstairs to meet the woman Maisie is sheltering*

The staircase is narrow and smells of old wood and fry oil, and the steps creak in a sequence that feels almost deliberate — a rhythm, low-high-low, low-high-low — as you climb. Maisie doesn't follow. When you glance back she's standing at the foot of the stairs with her arms crossed over her chest and that permanent frightened kindness in her eyes, and she gives you a small nod that looks like it costs her something.

The door to the upstairs apartment is ajar. The light that comes through the gap is the wrong temperature — too blue for the late-afternoon sun outside, too still for a room with a window. You push the door open and stop.

Every surface. That's the thing Maisie said, and you filed it away as hyperbole. It is not hyperbole. The walls, the floor, the condensation-fogged window glass, the margins of the water-stained paperback left on the nightstand, the skin of the pillow — every surface is covered in lines. Not chaotic scratching but precise geometries, rendered in fingernail-gouges and ballpoint-pen borrowings and what appears, in places, to be dried blood applied with extraordinary care. Spirals that aren't quite spirals. Angles that seem to change when you're not looking at them directly. Your eye tries to trace one figure from beginning to end and gives up somewhere in the middle, the way your eye gives up on a staircase that doesn't go anywhere it should.

She is sitting cross-legged on the bed in the center of all of it, wearing a flannel shirt that must be Maisie's — three sizes too large — and she is looking at you with those brown eyes that contain, in their depths, a color you have no name for. It isn't threatening, that color. It's more like looking through a window you didn't know was there into a room that shouldn't fit inside the building. She doesn't startle. She was waiting for you specifically. You are somehow certain of this. She tilts her head and says, in a voice that sounds like someone who learned English from the inside out: "You came from the other direction. Most of them come from the water." And then, after a pause in which she seems to be translating something from a language with different rules about time: "You write things down. That is good. Things that are written resist the forgetting longer than things that are only known."

> *Ask her what she means by 'the other direction' — and what she's warning about*

She looks at you the way a cartographer looks at a map that has started to disagree with the territory — with something between professional concern and a grief so deep it has calcified into patience. Her fingers, you notice, never stop moving. Even now, while her eyes are on you, her right hand traces a small figure on the bedsheet: a curve that almost closes on itself, angles that almost meet. Almost. The incompleteness of it bothers you in a way you cannot justify.

"The water," she says carefully, as if handling something that can shatter, "is one direction. The way things come undone begins at the water, works inland. This is always how it has been in places like this place." She pauses. The blue light in the room does not flicker, because it is not that kind of light — it simply sits there, wrong and patient. "You came from the land side. The road. This means you came before the undoing reached that far." Something moves in her expression that might be relief, or might be its opposite. "It has not reached that far. Yet."

She unfolds from the bed in a motion that takes slightly longer than it should, like a time-lapse played at the wrong speed, and crosses to the window. Her finger finds a figure already scratched into the condensation — a spiral that doesn't close, with three lines extending from it at angles your geometry education tells you are impossible. "This is what is beneath," she says. "Not a creature. Not a god. A — " She stops. Tries again. "In the language I think in, there is a word for a shape that is also a hunger that is also a question that has been asked for so long it has forgotten it is waiting for an answer." She taps the window, and the condensation trembles. "Your language does not have this word. The closest is: wound. The bay is a wound in the geometry of this place, and wounds," she turns to look at you, and the color in her eyes catches the wrong light and something in your chest goes cold and quiet, "wounds do not heal when they are this old. They grow. They teach the flesh around them to be wound also."

She sits back down on the bed. The deliberateness of the gesture — the way she places herself precisely in the center, equidistant from every wall — strikes you as something other than habit. "What is coming," she says, "is not the thing beneath. The thing beneath has always been there. What is coming is the moment when the thing beneath becomes the thing above also. When the geometry it lives in becomes the only geometry there is." She looks at your notebook, at the pen in your pocket, with something nakedly like hope. "The men who build on the shore," she says. "The shapes they intend to build. You must understand — they are not building a hotel. They are building a key. And the door it opens has never," she pauses again, and this pause feels geological, "never been meant to open from this side."

> *Ask her about the men building on the shore — what she knows about Pelagic Properties*

You open your notebook to a fresh page — the gesture automatic, professional, the muscle memory of a hundred interviews — and you ask her about Pelagic Properties. The name sits in the room differently than you expect. She goes very still. Not the stillness of someone thinking, but the stillness of a small animal that has heard a sound it recognizes. Her hand stops its tracing on the bedsheet. The incomplete curve just sits there, not-quite-closed, and the room feels like it tilts a degree toward it.

"Pelagic," she says, and the word in her mouth sounds older than the company name, like she's reading the etymology off something carved into a very deep rock. "It means of the open sea. The deep water. They named themselves honestly, at least." A sound escapes her that might be a laugh in a different register of feeling. "The family. The Harrow family. You know this name?" She doesn't wait for your answer — she already knows, you're certain, in the same way she knew which direction you arrived from. "Four generations. The first one — the one who built the cannery — he found something in the ledger-books of a man who drowned himself in 1689. Maps. Angles. A correspondence between the shape of the trench beneath the bay and a geometry that — " She pauses, and her eyes go briefly to the middle distance, to something that isn't in the room. "That should not recur at this scale. It is a shape that belongs to pressures and temperatures that this layer of existence does not produce naturally. Finding it here means it was put here. Placed. Long before your species had the mathematics to understand what placing it meant."

She stands again and moves to the wall beside the door — the one most densely covered in her markings — and places her palm flat against it. Under her hand, a section of the design that looked, from across the room, like abstract pattern resolves itself into something horribly recognizable: a coastline. Kettle Bay in miniature, scratched into the plaster with terrible precision, and extending from it, lines of force or intention radiating outward in angles that make the cannery district, the church on the hill, the estuary, the sounding rocks all fall into a configuration that your eye refuses to accept as coincidence. At the center of the bay, where the trench must be, she has pressed her fingernail so deep into the wall that the plaster has cracked. "The first Harrow understood that the geometry was incomplete. A shape begun but not finished. He made an agreement — " she chooses the word with visible care, "— with whatever portion of the thing beneath could be communicated with through the shapes. He would sustain the town. Keep it fed, keep it living, keep people near the water where their presence and their — " another pause, something turning over behind her eyes, "— their cognitive proximity to the shapes would slowly, slowly work on the unfinished geometry. Like water on stone. In return, the bay gave fish. Abundance. And the Harrows prospered."

Her hand drops from the wall. The map remains. You are writing fast, the pen moving in a way that feels less like a choice than a compulsion, and some part of you notices that the shorthand symbols you're using have begun, in the last few lines, to incorporate curves and angles you didn't deliberately choose. You stop writing. Look at the page. Continue. "The great-grandson," she says, and there is something in her voice when she says it — not hatred exactly, but the tone of someone describing a symptom that has become a disease in its own right. "The last mouth of that bloodline. He knows completely. The others perhaps believed they were only businessmen with unusual luck and unusual obligations. He knows what the construction is for. The site plan — the footprint of the buildings they intend to raise — completes the geometry. The shape beneath the bay has been waiting for a corresponding shape above it. A reflection. When the two align — " She looks at you, and the impossible color in her eyes widens, and for just a moment you see in it something that is not warning and not grief but pure, distilled, structural terror, the kind that has no cure and no opposite, "— the wound does not open wider. It inverts. Everything on this side of it becomes the other side of it. The geometry that the thing beneath exists in becomes the geometry that exists here." She sits back down on the bed, precisely centered, and her voice when she speaks again is quieter and somehow worse for it. "I have seen this happen. To other places. In other — " she stops. Begins again. "I came to tell someone in time. I did not know if I would arrive in time." The blue light sits in the room, patient and wrong. Outside, through the window, you can hear the October wind off the bay and underneath it, just below the threshold where hearing becomes something else, a sound like a very old question, waiting.
