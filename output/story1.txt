Dawn comes slowly to Tollmere, as if the marsh must be persuaded to release the night. Mist lies low over the
black water, pearl-gray and dense enough to soften every shape into rumor. Beneath your boots, the planks of
the Lantern Quay sweat river damp; they smell of tar, eel oil, wet reeds, and the faint sweetness of mushroom
smoke from cookfires just waking on the barge-houses. Somewhere out in the channels, a bell rings once and is
swallowed whole.

Mira Fenwake's sealed note is still warm from her hand where it rests in your satchel, though she gave it to
you before first light with the grave look of someone entrusting more than paper. Around you, Tollmere stirs:
a child coaxes embers under a tea brazier, a rope-pulley creaks, a heron lifts from a piling with a sound like

fog instead of working. On the water, a single lantern drifts past the pilings against the current, its flame
unwavering inside blue glass.

Then a second lantern appears beside it, and a third behind that—an orderly procession emerging from Fogmire
Channel, where no boat should be this hour. But there is no boat. Only lights, gliding toward you through the
mist as though carried by invisible hands.

You have been walking the Rust Marches for eleven days, and still the color of it strikes you — that deep,
arterial red-orange that stains the sky at the horizon line where the tall grasses bend and whisper against
each other with a sound like brushed silk. Your boots are lacquered bone, Corroden-made, and they have held up
better than the iron buckles on your pack, which crumbled to flakes on the third day. Everything metal dies
quickly here. You learned that lesson with the compass, too — its needle frozen at an angle that points toward
nothing you can name, its casing blooming with rust like a flower opening. You keep it anyway. The annotations
in your journal told you to.

Corren's Stake rises from the grassland like a splinter — a fortified outpost of glass-paned towers and
bone-frame buildings, built where a massive skethren pincer was driven into the earth generations ago as a
territorial marker. The outpost clings to it, structures lashed and bolted to the great curve of chitin. You
arrived at midday, sun-scorched and thirsty, and were met by Thessaly Drum-Walker, an elder whose name you'd
been given by a dying traveler four days east. She fed you. She let you rest. And then, as the afternoon light
turned the color of old blood, she told you about the disturbances to the south — temporal eddies forming at
Redsong Gulch, pools of frozen time appearing and vanishing like blisters on the landscape. She told you about
her sister, locked mid-step in the Stillfall for thirty-two years. She told you the disturbances feel the
same.

Now you stand at the outpost's southern gate, the red grass stretching before you in an endless, shivering
expanse. Your cracked compass needle twitches — not toward north, but toward something south, something
pulling. A young scavenger named Vesker leans against the gate frame, tuning fork in hand, watching you with
pale eyes that seem to be looking at where you'll be a half-second from now. "You feel it too," they say. It
is not a question. The grass bends south, though there is no wind.

Vesker walks slightly ahead of you, their gait oddly syncopated, as though they're stepping around obstacles

The Stillfall.

rhythm. A pulse. Something south of here is beating like a heart.

They are looking at where you are, fully present, fully frightened. "We should decide what we're walking into
before morning. Because I don't think Redsong Gulch is just a temporal disturbance. I think it's a door. And

The early pages are mundane. Supply lists, route notations, sketches of skethren anatomy. But halfway through,

You take first watch. It is the longest three hours of your life. The bioluminescent lantern pulses in
syncopation with the southern rhythm — blue-green, blue-green, dark, blue-green — and you find yourself
counting the intervals, mapping the pattern against the beat of your own heart. They do not align. They

nests behind your sternum and buzzes there like a wasp. Twice you catch the red grass at the edge of the
lantern light moving in ways that have nothing to do with wind — individual stalks bending, straightening,
bending again in sequence, tracing shapes that might be letters in a language written by something that thinks
in centuries. You do not read them. You grip your lacquered staff across your knees and watch the southern
dark and refuse to listen to the part of your mind that wants to hum along.

Vesker's shift is worse, though you only learn this in fragments. You sleep shallowly, dreaming of pendulums
and cracked glass, and wake twice — once to the sound of Vesker striking their tuning fork with desperate

subtle. You feel it through the soles of your boots, a steady *thrum-thrum-thrum* that vibrates up through

candle in a draft.

seen, fabrics that shimmer between threadbare and pristine depending on the angle. His edges flicker
constantly, a guttering silhouette, and his sharp-featured face wears an expression of studied, almost
theatrical, calm. He raises one hand — the motion stuttering, arriving before it begins — and speaks in a
voice that reaches you with a faint echo preceding it, his words arriving a half-breath before his lips form
them. "The Drum-Walker's errand runners," he says, and the smile that follows is thin as a wire. "I wondered
when she'd send someone. I've been sitting here for —" He pauses, tilts his head as if consulting some
internal clock. "Three days? Five hours? Time is somewhat academic this close to the rim." His flickering gaze
settles on Vesker, and something sharpens in it — recognition, not of the person, but of the *condition*.
"Oh," he says softly, and the theatrical affect drops for just a moment. "Oh, you poor creature. You're not
even a practitioner, are you? That's *natural* Drift." He takes a step closer, and the grass around his boots
ages and desiccates in a small circle, crisping to gray. "My name is Endric Vael. I was once of the Order of
the Unblinking, before they decided my methods were inconvenient. And I have been studying this gulch for
longer than I can reliably measure." His eyes move to you, calculating, the flicker at his edges intensifying.
"The question is whether you're here to help me, hinder me, or simply die in an interesting way. The gulch
accommodates all three."
